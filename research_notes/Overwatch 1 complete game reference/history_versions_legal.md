# Overwatch 1: history, build/version catalogue, legal and preservation landscape

Scope note: research notes from public sources, gathered 2026-10-15. This is not legal advice. Facts and court holdings are reported neutrally, and every claim has a URL. Where sources conflict or nothing was found, the notes say so.

## (A) History and timeline: development, announcement, betas, launch, heroes, systems, OW2 transition, shutdown

### Takeaway
Overwatch came out of the cancelled *Titan* MMO. It was announced at BlizzCon on 7 November 2014, entered closed beta on 27 October 2015, had an open beta from 5 to 9 May 2016 and launched on 24 May 2016. It grew from 21 launch heroes to 32 (Echo, April 2020), and no new OW1-era hero followed until OW2 development. Overwatch 2 was announced on 1 November 2019. OW1's last playable day was 2 October 2022, OW2 replaced it in place on 4 October 2022 with progress and cosmetics carried over, and OW2's promised PvE Hero Mode was cancelled on 16 May 2023.

### Inferences
- The OW1 executable was never offered again after the 2–3 October 2022 cutover: OW2 replaced it in the same Battle.net product slot. Any OW1 client today is a user-retained copy, which matters for the "lawfully acquired" and "complete game" questions in Part C.
- Echo (April 2020) was the last hero designed for OW1's 6v6 live game. A 1.74-era client should contain the 32-hero roster, which matches the project's observed hero-select roster of 32. Sojourn and Junker Queen were OW2-beta-only and should be absent.

## (B) Build/version catalogue: known OW1 version and build numbers; what 1.74.0.0.104319 corresponds to

### Takeaway
OW1 PC versions follow `title.major.minor.platform.build`, where the last field is a monotonically increasing build counter. Anchors found:
- 0.8.0.0.24919: closed beta release, 27 October 2015.
- 1.4.0.2.32143: October 2016 patch.

No public source found in this session gives a release date for **1.74.0.0.104319**. Community projects and this project treat it as the final OW1 PC client, which fits the last OW1 retail patch (6 September 2022), but that is unproven. Console builds used different numbering (e.g. "2.65").

### Cited Findings
- Version scheme, per a Blizzard forum reply: title.major_content_release.minor_content_release.platform_compatibility.build_number. The build number is a unique identifier that increments with every build made, so it is not tied to a headline version. — [search summary citing Blizzard forum "What patch will shock vs the world be played on?"](https://us.forums.blizzard.com/en/overwatch/t/what-patch-will-shock-vs-the-world-be-played-on/587881)
- **0.8.0.0.24919**: "Patch 0.8 Beta Release", implemented 27 October 2015 (closed beta start). — [GosuGamers](https://www.gosugamers.net/overwatch/news/33146-overwatch-patch-notes-0-8-0)
- **1.4.0.2.32143**: October 2016 PC patch (released ~12 October 2016, 1.74 GB download). Secondary source; the "1.74" there is a file size, not a version. — [iTechPost](https://www.itechpost.com/articles/39444/20161011/overwatch-news-updates-new-1-74gb-patch-finally-coming-out-oct-12-heres-what-you-can-expect.htm)

### Inferences
- Build counter growth: 24919 (Oct 2015) to 32143 (Oct 2016) is about 7,200 builds a year. At a similar rate, 104319 would land around 2021–2022, which is consistent with a final-year OW1 build. This is arithmetic, not evidence.
- Major version 74 means roughly 74 content releases after 1.0. That is plausible for about 6.3 years of patches and hotfixes (1.4 in Oct 2016).
- 1.74.0.0.104319 is most plausibly the build live at the end of OW1's life: the 6 Sep 2022 retail patch or a later hotfix before the 2–3 Oct 2022 shutdown. The project should describe it as "the final OW1 PC client as distributed by the community / user-retained" unless a CDN version history confirms the date.
- Platform field 0 in "1.74.0.0" against 2 in "1.4.0.2" suggests the platform/compatibility field changed meaning or value over time. It is unresolved.

## (C) Legal and preservation landscape: DMCA §1201, EU Stop Killing Games, server-emulation cases, Blizzard EULA, AGPL, VGHF

### Takeaway
US §1201 exemptions, as renewed in 2024, permit circumvention to restore *personal, local* play of lawfully acquired "complete games" whose servers were shut down. They also permit on-premises preservation by eligible libraries, archives and museums. The 2024 rulemaking **rejected remote access** for institutions. The 8th Circuit in *Davidson v. Jung* (2005) held that a Blizzard EULA/ToU ban on reverse engineering is enforceable, waives the §1201(f) interoperability exception, and that the bnetd Battle.net emulator violated §1201. Blizzard's current EULA expressly bans reverse engineering, protocol emulation/redirection and connections to emulated servers. Enforcement history ranges from cease-and-desists (Nostalrius 2016) to police action (Club Penguin Rewritten 2022) to licensing (City of Heroes Homecoming 2024). The EU "Stop Destroying Videogames" ECI collected about 1.29 million verified signatures, but on 16 June 2026 the Commission declined binding legislation.

### Cited Findings
**US DMCA §1201 exemption, current text (37 CFR 201.40(b)(19), last amended 89 FR 85446, 28 Oct 2024)** — [eCFR 37 CFR 201.40](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-37?part=201&section=201.40)
- (b)(19)(i), games "lawfully acquired as complete games" whose owner has "ceased to provide access" to the external server. Two permitted uses:
  - (A) copying and modifying "to restore access to the game for personal, local gameplay on a personal computer or video game console";
  - (B) preservation "in a playable form by an eligible library, archives, or museum", without commercial advantage, and the game "is not distributed or made available outside of the physical premises" of the institution.
- (b)(19)(ii) covers preservation of games that do not need a server and are "no longer reasonably available in the commercial marketplace", by institutions only and on premises. (b)(19)(iii) covers console OS software as needed.
- Definitions:
  - "Complete games": "(A)" games playable "without accessing or reproducing copyrightable content stored or previously stored on an external computer server." Part (B) extends this, for institutions, to games whose content includes server-stored code; the exact (B) text needs verification.
  - "Ceased to provide access": an affirmative statement of discontinuation, or at least six months of discontinued support that has not been restored.
  - "Local gameplay": play on a PC or console or locally connected devices, "and not through an online service or facility."
  - Eligible institution: collections open to the public or to researchers, public-service mission, trained staff, lawfully acquired materials, digital security.
- The text does not mention online multiplayer explicitly. The online limit comes from the "local gameplay" definition.

**2024 rulemaking: remote access denied**
- The Copyright Office declined a new exemption that VGHF and the Software Preservation Network sought. It would have let libraries and archives circumvent so they could give researchers *remote* access to out-of-print games. The Register sided with the ESA, which warned of an "online arcade" harming the reissue market. The rejection is at p. 191 of the Register's Recommendation. — [InfoDocket, 25 Oct 2024](https://www.infodocket.com/2024/10/25/u-s-copyright-office-rejects-dmca-exemption-to-support-game-preservation/); [UBC Allard IP law](https://iplaw.allard.ubc.ca/?p=13285)
- VGHF's statement (25 Oct 2024) cites its study finding about **87%** of US games released before 2010 out of print. VGHF was disappointed but had "no regrets", and it criticised the ESA's opposition to remote research access "under any conditions" as pushing researchers to extra-legal methods. It plans to keep advocating and to work with industry members. — [VGHF statement](https://gamehistory.org/dmca-2024-statement/)

**Davidson & Associates (Blizzard) v. Jung, 422 F.3d 630 (8th Cir. 2005), the bnetd case**
- Blizzard and Vivendi sued the bnetd.org developers, whose Battle.net emulator let Blizzard games play online without Battle.net CD-key authentication. The court affirmed summary judgment for Blizzard. — [Opinion, law.resource.org](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/422/422.F3d.630.04-3654.html); [Harvard copy](https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/tfisher/IP/2005_Blizzard.pdf)
- Holdings, per the opinion and summaries:
  - The EULA and ToU were enforceable contracts banning reverse engineering, and they were not preempted by the Copyright Act.
  - By agreeing, the defendants waived the reverse-engineering and interoperability (§1201(f)) defence.
  - bnetd circumvented Battle.net's access control and violated the anti-circumvention and anti-trafficking provisions; §1201(f) did not apply because the use was not solely for interoperability and enabled unauthorised access.
  — [Opinion](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/422/422.F3d.630.04-3654.html); [Open Casebook](https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/739-tlc-seminar-materials/as-printable-html/33/); [Cardozo AELJ article](https://cardozoaelj.com/wp-content/uploads/Journal%20Issues/Volume%2024/Issue%201/Kinitsky.pdf)

**Blizzard EULA (last revised 21 March 2024)** — [Blizzard End User License Agreement](https://www.blizzard.com/en-us/legal/fba4d00f-c7e4-4883-b8b9-1b4500a402ea/blizzard-end-user-license-agreement)
- §1.C.i prohibits: "reverse engineer, derive source code from, modify, disassemble, decompile, or create derivative works based on or related to the Platform."
- §1.C.viii prohibits: "intercept, emulate or redirect the communication protocols used by Blizzard in any way, for any purpose."
- §1.C.ix(i) prohibits "any connection to any unauthorized server that emulates, or attempts to emulate, the Platform". §1.C.ix(ii) prohibits third-party programs or tools not expressly authorised.
- §1.C.ii bans cheats and bots. §1.C.vi bans unauthorised data-mining or intercepting software.
- There is no express interoperability carve-out.
- Delaware law. US residents go to binding individual AAA arbitration; non-arbitrable claims go to the courts of Orange County, California.

**Blizzard v. Bossland (C.D. Cal., 2017)**

### Inferences
These are not legal advice.
- OW1 almost certainly fails the "complete game" (A) definition, because gameplay needs content and logic from Blizzard servers. A project that *reimplements* the servers falls outside the personal "local gameplay" restoration exemption as written. The project's own loopback-only, no-Internet/LAN design mirrors the "local gameplay" concept, but that alignment is not a legal safe harbour.
- The (b)(19)(i)(B) and (ii) preservation paths apply only to *eligible institutions* on premises. An individual open-source project is not covered, and remote or online play is explicitly not covered after 2024.
- *Davidson v. Jung* is the most directly relevant precedent. It concerned a Battle.net-protocol emulator, held that the EULA waived §1201(f), and found circumvention of Battle.net authentication. The present project's choices are risk-relevant facts under that holding's reasoning:
  - generating its own certificates and keys instead of shipping Blizzard's;
  - keeping original checks enabled;
  - not distributing client bytes, keys or dumps.
- Blizzard's EULA §1.C.viii ("emulate or redirect the communication protocols ... for any purpose") and §1.C.ix (emulated servers) directly cover this kind of project as a contractual matter. Contract claims bind parties who accepted the EULA.
- AGPL-3.0 licenses only the project's own code. It cannot license Blizzard IP and gives no protection against Blizzard's copyright or EULA claims. Its network-use clause (§13) means any public server deployment must offer its source.
- The enforcement spectrum runs from cease-and-desist (Nostalrius) through criminal referral (Club Penguin Rewritten, which involved distribution and reportedly ad revenue) to licensing (Homecoming). Commercial activity and distributing copyrighted assets appear to be the aggravating factors in reported cases.
- The EU outcome (June 2026) creates no legal right to keep or revive OW1. The promised voluntary code of conduct is forward-looking.
